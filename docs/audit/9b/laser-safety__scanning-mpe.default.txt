A beam that sweeps across the eye delivers one pulse each time it crosses the pupil, and ICNIRP 2013 (Health Phys. 105:271, p. 287) applies its repetitive-pulse rules to it: (1) each pass within the limit for one pulse of its duration, (2) every group of passes within the limit for the time it spans, up to the whole exposure (the average power), (3) for the retinal thermal limit, each pass within C_P times the single-pass limit. The pass lasts from half power to half power through the aperture (W/v, about 7 mm/v for a beam narrower than the pupil); its energy is all the light that gets through. The cornea's 1 mm aperture sees a shorter pass than the retina's 7 mm one. A beam under 1 mm (1/e diameter) is held to its peak instead: on the cornea below 0.35 s (ICNIRP Table 5 note c) and, in the infrared, on the anterior segment (Table 7 note b with note d). Each pass then lasts d·√(ln 2/2)/v at a point on the line the beam axis sweeps, and delivers P·√(8/π)/(d·v) there.

The beam is taken to sweep through the centre of the pupil, with the eye on the centre of the scan line and focused on the scanner, so the retinal image stays put and every pass lands on the same spot (IEC 60825-1:2014's default evaluation of scanned emission; Schulmeister 2025). Speed ω·r at distance r from the pivot: ω = Θ·f for one direction, 2Θ·f for both. Use 0.25 s for an accidental glance at a visible beam and 10 s or more for invisible ones. Not modelled: the turning points of a scan (slower, with passes in pairs), sinusoidal and raster scans, pulsed lasers (each pass a group of pulses), and a moving retinal image, which is less restrictive.

If the scanner stops, the beam is stationary: "If the scan stops" above gives the time a stopped beam takes to exceed the limits, which bounds the reaction time of the scan-failure safeguard that IEC 60825-1 requires when the classification relies on the scan.
